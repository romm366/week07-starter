# Provisional AI themes: grocery-shopping label claims

## Scope and evidence status

Research question: How do everyday shoppers make food choices when they see claims such as "sugar free", "light" or "no added sugar" and have only a few seconds? What do they look at, and when do they feel unsure or misled?

Risky assumption to examine: Labels can be dangerous and shape shoppers' decisions.

This analysis uses the three grocery interview records in `evidence.csv`: I9 / INT09 / P9, I10 / INT10 / P10 and I11 / INT11 / P11. Their record text matches the corresponding paraphrases in `practice-notes.md`. These are two representations of the same interviews, not independent corroboration.

**Mixed source status:** `practice-notes.md` explicitly labels the equipment-borrowing material I1–I8 and O1–O2 as **FICTIONAL PRACTICE** and separates the user-supplied grocery interviews from it. All fictional practice equipment material is excluded. The grocery section is not labelled fictional; that distinction is preserved here without independently verifying its provenance.

All five themes below are provisional interpretations. Routine shopping actions, beliefs and feelings are reported through interview paraphrases. Product-task choices are documented within those paraphrases. No grocery-shopping observation records, timed choices or gaze measurements are supplied. A task preference is not an observed purchase.

## 1. Rushing can reduce ingredient checking, while reported checking priorities differ

**Supporting IDs and reported behaviour**

- **I9:** Reports checking dates and shelf life first, trying to check ingredients, and skipping ingredients when rushed (`practice-notes.md:56`).
- **I10:** Reports looking early at nutrition information on the back and checking producer and country for unfamiliar products (`practice-notes.md:72`).
- **I11:** Reports looking at the front label, ingredients and producing country, and almost never reading the product description (`practice-notes.md:90`).

**Contrary or qualifying IDs:** **I10** says speed is generally not important in shopping (`practice-notes.md:66`). This qualifies any claim that time pressure is a shared condition. I10 and I11 also report checking detailed information; the evidence does not support universal reliance on front claims.

**Observed behaviour:** None supplied for grocery shopping. The stated checking sequences are self-reports.

**What the notes cannot establish:** Actual first glances, time spent reading, what is noticed within a few seconds, or how often rushing changes a purchase. I9 supplies a reported link between rushing and omitted checks, not a measured effect of time pressure.

## 2. Familiarity and producer trust can reduce the perceived need for detailed checks

**Supporting IDs and reported behaviour**

- **I9:** Assumes she knows the contents of familiar basic foods and describes habitual purchases (`practice-notes.md:50,56`).
- **I10:** Prefers familiar, trusted producers, generally trusts labels without checking every detail or comparing several similar products, and checks producer and origin for unfamiliar products (`practice-notes.md:66,72`).

**Contrary or qualifying IDs:** **I9** distrusts front-of-pack imagery and tries to check ingredients (`practice-notes.md:56`). **I11** checks ingredients and doubts health marketing on protein bars (`practice-notes.md:90`). Trust therefore varies by source and situation within these accounts.

**Observed behaviour:** None supplied. Familiarity, trust and reduced checking are reported rather than independently observed.

**What the notes cannot establish:** Whether familiar products are understood accurately, whether producer trust causes fewer checks, or whether trust makes someone more likely to be misled. Familiarity with a food and trust in a producer should not be assumed to be identical.

## 3. Sugar-related wording contributes to stated health judgments

**Supporting IDs and documented task responses**

- **I10:** Interprets "light" as less sugar, distinguishes no added sugar from remaining natural sugars, and explains choosing Coca-Cola Zero as healthier mainly through the large Zero Sugar label (`practice-notes.md:70,74`).
- **I11:** Also interprets "light" as less sugar and "sugar free" as no sugar. Explains the Coca-Cola Zero choice through a belief about the quantity of sugar substitutes relative to sugar in regular Coca-Cola (`practice-notes.md:88,92`). This reasoning differs from I10's explanation.

**Contrary or qualifying IDs:** **I9** does not regard no added sugar as automatically healthy and prefers sugar to artificial sweeteners (`practice-notes.md:54`). This challenges a general claim that sugar-reduction wording always produces a favourable health judgment.

**Observed behaviour:** The interviews document task choices and explanations. No independent observation of label attention or an everyday purchase is supplied.

**What the notes cannot establish:** Correct regulatory meanings, the products' comparative health effects, or whether wording caused a choice. No timed or controlled label comparison is documented. Participant interpretations are not verified label definitions.

## 4. Lower sugar alone does not determine the preferred food

**Supporting IDs and reported behaviour**

- **I9:** Reports choosing discounted products and comparing unit prices. In the yogurt task, ranks Skyr above FIT and says she would not buy FIT because she dislikes sweeteners, although the supplied comparison lists FIT as lower in sugar. The sugar figures are unverified task material (`practice-notes.md:50,58`).
- **I10:** Explains preferring plain Greek yogurt partly through its use as a basic meal ingredient, while viewing flavoured yogurt as a convenient sweet snack (`practice-notes.md:76`).
- **I11:** Describes the importance of habit, health, taste, quality and circumstances in shopping priorities (`practice-notes.md:84`).

**Contrary or qualifying IDs:** **I10** gives the Zero Sugar label as the main reason for the cola health judgment, and **I11** also uses sugar/substitute reasoning in that task (`practice-notes.md:74,92`). Sugar-related considerations can matter strongly in a particular comparison.

**Observed behaviour:** None supplied for purchases. Rankings, intended avoidance and habitual choices are recorded in interview paraphrases.

**What the notes cannot establish:** The relative weight of price, taste, convenience or claims in actual purchases, or how those weights change under a seconds-long deadline. A health ranking, stated preference and purchase decision are different outcomes.

## 5. Doubt appears around perceived inconsistencies and uncertain product knowledge

**Supporting IDs and reported experiences**

- **I9:** Reports an apparent mismatch between a bread claim and its ingredient list, distrust of imagery, uncertainty about Nutri-Score accuracy, and a wish for honest information (`practice-notes.md:56,60`).
- **I11:** Questions protein-bar health marketing and expresses uncertainty about recalled information behind a product choice (`practice-notes.md:90,92`). Also challenges the corn comparison, saying both products were in water and there was no vacuum-sealed option (`practice-notes.md:94`).

**Contrary or qualifying IDs:** **I10** generally trusts labels and says the task nutrition information matched expectations or did not surprise her (`practice-notes.md:72,74,76`). **I11** also says she was not very surprised overall (`practice-notes.md:94`).

**Observed behaviour:** No direct observation of uncertainty or a feeling of being misled is supplied. These are paraphrased reports of doubt, distrust and task criticism.

**What the notes cannot establish:** Whether any packaging was actually misleading, when a feeling of being misled arose, or whether sugar claims specifically produced it. The bread example concerns a different claim. The corn discrepancy may concern the task materials; it cannot be treated as established packaging deception.

## Data quality and missing information

- **Source availability:** `sources/notes.md` is absent. The grocery sections of `practice-notes.md` provide the matching source notes.
- **Evidence structure:** The CSV has three unique evidence IDs and encounters, all marked interview, with no empty fields. Each record combines several topics. Repeated supporting IDs across themes do not add participants or establish prevalence.
- **Paraphrase and collection gaps:** Full transcripts, exact participant wording, collection dates and locations are not supplied. Grocery recruitment and the complete questioning/task protocol are not established by these inputs. The fictional practice recruitment description must not be transferred to the grocery interviews.
- **Attention and time gaps:** No timed grocery decisions, gaze records, observed label-reading sequences or linked purchase outcomes are supplied. What shoppers actually look at within a few seconds remains unanswered.
- **Task integrity:** I11's corn comparison is internally disputed, and the product images are absent (`practice-notes.md:92,94`). Exact products, stimuli and corrected task information are needed. Extra product-comparison commentary is not participant testimony or verified evidence.
- **Nutrition information:** I9's figures are supplied task material, not independently verified data. Exact figures are missing for I10's cola and yogurt comparisons. No label definitions, nutrition claims or health conclusions were externally checked.
- **Missing responses:** I10 lacks a specific last-trip account; I11 lacks the final answer about anything missed (`practice-notes.md:64,96`). Missing information is not a negative answer.
- **Identifying fields:** Identifying attribution/context details occur in the CSV `context` column and the I9–I11 source headings. Analysis of those fields was set aside; their contents are not reproduced here. The non-identifying evidence records supply the themes.
- **Unresolved questions:** How I9 weighs sugar against sweeteners, price and convenience for unfamiliar products; how I10's producer trust affects nutrition checking for unfamiliar products; and which corn products I11 saw remain open, as recorded in the CSV.

## Implication for the risky assumption

The interviews provide limited evidence that label wording enters stated health judgments and that perceived inconsistencies accompany distrust. They also contain selective trust, rejection of an automatic no-added-sugar health inference, and choices involving other priorities. They do not establish that labels are dangerous, cause harm, or cause changes in real purchases under time pressure. The themes describe these accounts and are hypotheses for further checking, not population findings.
